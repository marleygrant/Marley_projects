Stopping Fraud Without Stalling Growth: Bank Fraud Policy

2026 Wake Forest MSBA Analytics Challenge, presented to a panel of judges, with Karis Avant, Micaela Creo, Andrew Nguyen & Chris Tsimbinos
71% of Fraud Stopped $4.9M Est. Annual Savings 285K Applications

Deacon Financial Services (DFS), the bank in our case, launched its "New Money Bonus" campaign: a $200 sign-up bonus plus referral rewards. It brought in 146,181 applications from July to October, the bank's first real digital growth in two years. Estimated fraud also rose from about 340 to about 2,960 cases a month. The bank's current fraud score stopped only 16% of fraud and held 19% of applicants for manual review, which broke its "open in minutes" promise. Fraud losses and reviews together cost an estimated $13.1M a year. Our question: how can the bank limit fraud without stalling growth?

My own model. Before the team model came together, I built my own model on my own to work through the problem end to end. It's a stepwise logistic regression that scores each application when it's submitted, plus a second version that adds the first 30 days of account activity. Every cleaning step (missing-value medians, outlier caps, low-variability checks) was learned from training rows only, and I checked it with 5-fold cross-validation. I compared it head to head with the bank's live score using actual losses from its investigation records: net value = fraud caught × loss per missed fraud − applications flagged × review cost. I ran that comparison at equal review capacity, and again with a fuller cost that adds bonuses paid and investigation time. To make sure the gap wasn't luck, I re-drew the test set 1,000 times.

My model reached a test AUC of 0.740, against 0.640 for the bank's score on the same test applications. Reviewing the same share of applications as the bank, my model caught 55.5% of fraud versus 34.1% for the bank's score, and came out about $379K ahead per 100,000 applications while the bank's score lost about $203K, once all costs of a missed fraud are counted (direct loss, bonus paid and investigation). My model beat the bank's score in all 1,000 re-draws. That showed me how much better the data could do than the current score, and the team's model pushed it further.

The team model we presented. We pulled 30 tables from the bank's SQL database and merged them into one row per application: 285,046 applications and 14,325 confirmed fraud cases. Much of the work was thinking like the people committing the fraud. How would someone farm the $200 bonus, open accounts with stolen or made-up identities, or run a referral ring? Those questions turned into new variables the raw data didn't have, such as how many applications came from the same device, referral code or email domain in the last 30 days, hours since the same person last applied, and how many seconds a session took to submit. Our "ring" signals proved the point: the top 1% of repeated devices accounted for 54% of device-linked fraud, and 8 of the model's top 15 drivers were variables we engineered this way. Because fraudsters adapt, we framed this as an ongoing habit: keep asking how someone would game the next offer, and build the variable before the losses show up. We trained on January 2025 to June 2026, validated on July and tested on August, so fraud rings couldn't leak between training and test. We compared logistic regression, random forest and gradient boosting. Gradient boosting won with a test ROC-AUC of 0.889, against 0.607 for the bank's current score.

We recommended a three-band policy built on the team model. Applications under an 11% fraud probability are approved instantly (81.6%), those from 11% to 17% go to human review (6.6%, about 80 a day and under 5% of review capacity), and those at 17% or higher are declined. The policy stops 71% of fraud versus 16% today, about 4.5× more. It cuts the estimated yearly net cost from $13.1M to $8.2M, saving about $4.9M a year, and raises instant decisions from about 81% to 93%. A decline-only policy would have saved about $1.4M more. We kept human review anyway because it lets through 21% fewer frauds than a flat approve/decline rule and keeps producing fresh fraud labels to retrain the model. We also gave Marketing and Risk recommendations: referrals were 13% of applications but 41% of fraud, so we proposed a repeat-device and referral-code review rule and a bonus clawback clause.

Business impact: gives the bank a way to keep its growth campaign running while catching far more fraud, and lets most good customers still open an account in minutes.

ECG Patient Matching: Hospital ECG vs. Apple Watch

2026 Wake Forest School of Medicine Build-A-Thon — Team AI Deacons, with Brian Mejia-Lopez, Nathan Potisit & Amy Platter
🥈 2nd Place 0.984 AUROC 37K+ ECGs

Doctors want to monitor heart health at home with smartwatch ECGs, but that only works if each watch recording can be trusted to belong to the right patient. The challenge: given two single-lead (Lead I) ECGs, one from a hospital machine and one from an Apple Watch or a second hospital ECG, score from 0 to 1 how likely they came from the same person. Entries were judged by AUROC on a Wake Forest holdout of patients no team ever saw.

We built a three-step pipeline in Python: clean every ECG the same way (resample to 250 Hz, filter drift and noise, rescale so the beat's shape matters, not the device), score each pair with two models, then blend them. A Siamese CNN turns each 10-second signal into a 64-number "fingerprint" and compares them (80% of the score); a heartbeat model compares the median beat plus six measurements a doctor can check, like heart rate, R peak height and T wave height (20%). We trained on 37,237 hospital ECGs from 10,000 MIMIC-IV patients plus St. Jude hospital/watch pairs, split patients before building pairs so no one appeared in both training and testing, and tuned every setting on a separate practice group.

Our model came in second place at the Build-A-Thon. On patients it never saw, it reached an AUROC of 0.984 for hospital vs. Apple Watch and 0.981 for hospital vs. hospital, and ranked the patient's own watch ECG first out of 48 candidates 79.2% of the time (chance is about 2%). Error analysis showed most misses came from noisy watch recordings or large heart-rate changes between recordings, and we tested several "fixes" before trusting them, keeping only what actually helped.

Business impact: shows a smartwatch ECG carries a personal signature strong enough to flag records filed under the wrong patient and to audit "anonymized" ECG data for re-identification risk before it's shared.

Regional Hospital Patient Volume Forecast

Team project — with Connor Farrell & Jadyn Jeanes
3-Month Forecast 28 Months of Data

A regional hospital's leadership team needed a data-driven read on patient volume to plan staffing and resources heading into the winter months.

Helped isolate day-of-week and monthly seasonality from 28 months of daily patient census data using PivotTable-derived seasonal indices, built the deseasonalized regression trendline, then reseasonalized the results to produce the final forecast.

Delivered a three-month (Nov–Jan) forecast identifying Wednesdays and Thursdays as peak-demand days and a January volume spike tied to post-holiday visits and flu season.

Business impact: directly informed staffing, scheduling, and supply-chain recommendations for hospital leadership.

Econometric Analysis of Global Life Expectancy

Independent capstone project
10+ Models Compared 77% Adj. R²

Analyzed panel data from 150+ countries to identify which economic, social, and health factors most reliably predict national life expectancy.

Built and compared 10+ regression specifications in R — testing interaction terms and nonlinear effects, then applying Feasible Generalized Least Squares (FGLS) correction to address heteroskedasticity across the dataset.

The final model reached 77% adjusted R², isolating education, mortality, GDP per capita, and resource access as the strongest levers.

Business impact: translated into clear, decision-ready policy recommendations rather than raw regression tables.
