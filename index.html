ECG Patient Matching: Hospital ECG vs. Apple Watch

2026 Wake Forest School of Medicine Build-A-Thon — Team AI Deacons, with Brian Mejia-Lopez, Nathan Potisit & Amy Platter
🥈 2nd Place 0.984 AUROC 37K+ ECGs

Doctors want to monitor heart health at home with smartwatch ECGs, but that only works if each watch recording can be trusted to belong to the right patient. The challenge: given two single-lead (Lead I) ECGs, one from a hospital machine and one from an Apple Watch or a second hospital ECG, score from 0 to 1 how likely they came from the same person. Entries were judged by AUROC on a Wake Forest holdout of patients no team ever saw.

We built a three-step pipeline in Python: clean every ECG the same way (resample to 250 Hz, filter drift and noise, rescale so the beat's shape matters, not the device), score each pair with two models, then blend them. A Siamese CNN turns each 10-second signal into a 64-number "fingerprint" and compares them (80% of the score); a heartbeat model compares the median beat plus six measurements a doctor can check, like heart rate, R peak height and T wave height (20%). We trained on 37,237 hospital ECGs from 10,000 MIMIC-IV patients plus St. Jude hospital/watch pairs, split patients before building pairs so no one appeared in both training and testing, and tuned every setting on a separate practice group.

Our model came in second place at the Build-A-Thon. On patients it never saw, it reached an AUROC of 0.984 for hospital vs. Apple Watch and 0.981 for hospital vs. hospital, and ranked the patient's own watch ECG first out of 48 candidates 79.2% of the time (chance is about 2%). Error analysis showed most misses came from noisy watch recordings or large heart-rate changes between recordings, and we tested several "fixes" before trusting them, keeping only what actually helped.

Business impact: shows a smartwatch ECG carries a personal signature strong enough to flag records filed under the wrong patient and to audit "anonymized" ECG data for re-identification risk before it's shared.

Regional Hospital Patient Volume Forecast

Team project — with Connor Farrell & Jadyn Jeanes
3-Month Forecast 28 Months of Data

A regional hospital's leadership team needed a data-driven read on patient volume to plan staffing and resources heading into the winter months.

Helped isolate day-of-week and monthly seasonality from 28 months of daily patient census data using PivotTable-derived seasonal indices, built the deseasonalized regression trendline, then reseasonalized the results to produce the final forecast.

Delivered a three-month (Nov–Jan) forecast identifying Wednesdays and Thursdays as peak-demand days and a January volume spike tied to post-holiday visits and flu season.

Business impact: directly informed staffing, scheduling, and supply-chain recommendations for hospital leadership.

Econometric Analysis of Global Life Expectancy

Independent capstone project
10+ Models Compared 77% Adj. R²

Analyzed panel data from 150+ countries to identify which economic, social, and health factors most reliably predict national life expectancy.

Built and compared 10+ regression specifications in R — testing interaction terms and nonlinear effects, then applying Feasible Generalized Least Squares (FGLS) correction to address heteroskedasticity across the dataset.

The final model reached 77% adjusted R², isolating education, mortality, GDP per capita, and resource access as the strongest levers.

Business impact: translated into clear, decision-ready policy recommendations rather than raw regression tables.
